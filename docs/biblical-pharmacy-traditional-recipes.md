## A. How-to primer: common preparations & safety basics

### Shared safety basics (read before any recipe)

1. **Clean tools.** Wash hands, jars, lids, spoons, and strainers. For alcohol or vinegar extracts, start with dry herbs and dry jars to reduce spoilage risk.
2. **Label everything.** Name, ingredients, date made, alcohol % if applicable, and “external only” when true. Unlabeled jars are how accidents happen.
3. **Know your plant.** Use culinary-grade or correctly identified dried herbs from reputable suppliers. Never forage unless you are trained; look-alikes can be toxic.
4. **Start low.** First use: small amount, watch for allergy (itching, swelling, hives, breathing trouble → emergency care).
5. **Pregnancy, nursing, children.** Many herbs are **not** appropriate. When a recipe does not explicitly say “food-level kitchen spice only,” assume **ask a clinician first**. Honey is **never** for infants under 1 year (botulism risk).
6. **Medicines.** Herbs can interact with drugs. **Never stop prescribed medicines** based on this document—ask a pharmacist or clinician.
7. **Essential oils (EO).** Not the same as dried herbs in tea. **Do not** casually ingest EOs. For topical use, dilute heavily in a carrier oil and patch-test. Keep away from eyes, inner ears, mucous membranes, pets’ cages, and unsupervised children.
8. **When to discard.** Mold, off smells, fizzing that shouldn’t be there, cloudy oil that was clear, or past a sensible shelf window → discard. When unsure, throw it out.
9. **When to see a clinician.** High fever, trouble breathing, chest pain, severe dehydration, bloody stool/vomit, unexplained weight loss, wounds that worsen, suspected poisoning, psychiatric crisis, or any symptom that frightens you. Herbal kitchen craft is **not** urgent care.

### Infusion (tea — delicate parts)

**What it is:** Hot water poured over leaves, flowers, or soft plant parts; steeped covered.

**Basic method:** Use about **1–2 teaspoons dried herb** (or a small handful of fresh) per **8 oz (240 ml)** near-boiling water. Cover 5–15 minutes. Strain.

**Safety:** Covering keeps aromatic oils in the cup. Do not assume “natural = unlimited.” Discard leftover tea after a day in the fridge, or sooner if it smells off.

### Decoction (simmer — tougher parts)

**What it is:** Roots, barks, seeds, or woody material simmered in water.

**Basic method:** About **1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon dried hard material** per cup water; bring to a boil, then gentle simmer **10–20 minutes** covered; strain.

**Safety:** Longer heat extracts more constituents—including bitter or irritating ones. Do not decoct delicate flowers the same way (they often turn flat or harsh).

### Tincture (alcohol extract)

**What it is:** Plant material macerated in ethanol (or ethanol–water) for weeks, then pressed/strained.

**Educational kitchen pattern (adults):** Often roughly **1 part dried herb by weight to 5 parts liquid by volume** (a common folk “1:5”), using **40–60% alcohol** (80–120 proof vodka-class spirits) for many leafy/aromatic herbs. Roots may need higher proof in professional practice—**this primer does not train you as a pharmacist.**

**Steps sketch:** Chop/crush herb → jar → cover completely with alcohol → label → shake daily → wait **2–6 weeks** → strain → bottle in dropper bottles → label with alcohol % and date.

**Safety:** Keep away from children. Not for people who must avoid alcohol. Pregnancy: generally avoid medicinal tinctures unless a clinician directs. Shelf life of a well-made high-proof tincture is often **years** if stored cool/dark; discard if cloudy with growth or strange odor. **Glycerites** (glycerin extracts) are a different method with shorter shelf life—study before substituting.

### Syrup (honey or sugar + strong tea/decoction)

**What it is:** A sweetened, shelf-stabilized liquid for pleasant sipping (food tradition).

**Safety:** Refrigerate homemade syrups. Typical home fridge life is often discussed as **weeks to a few months** depending on sugar/honey ratio and cleanliness—**when in doubt, discard**. **No honey for infants under 1.** Diabetics: syrups are sugar—ask a clinician. Syrups are **food vehicles**, not disease cures.

### Salve / ointment (oil + beeswax)

**What it is:** Herbal-infused oil thickened with beeswax for external skin application.

**Basic ratio literacy:** Many home crafters start near **1 oz (28 g) beeswax per 1 cup (240 ml) infused oil**, then adjust firmer/softer. Melt gently, pour into tins, cool, label **EXTERNAL**.

**Safety:** Patch-test. Do not put salves in eyes, deep puncture wounds, or infected wounds that need medical care. Beeswax allergy is possible. Keep lids clean.

### Poultice (fresh or moistened herb on skin)

**What it is:** Crushed moist plant applied externally, often with a clean cloth.

**Safety:** Short contact; watch for irritation. Do not poultice over broken skin with unknown wild plants. Remove if burning or blistering begins.

### Vinegar (acetic extracts / oxymels)

**What it is:** Herbs steeped in apple-cider or wine vinegar; sometimes honey is added later (oxymel tradition).

**Safety:** Use food-grade vinegar (~5% acetic acid). Avoid metal lids contacting vinegar long-term (use plastic-lined or glass). Refrigerate if you add fresh garlic/onion or dilute with water/honey. Culinary vinegars are not plague cures or disinfectant substitutes for clinical infection.

## B. Four-step guidance for combining herbs thoughtfully

This is **synergy literacy**, not a license to invent “miracle stacks.”

### Step 1 — Name one clear traditional purpose
Example: “after-meal settling tea,” not “detox your liver and kill pathogens.” Vague mega-goals invite unsafe stacking.

### Step 2 — Choose a simple “lead” herb you already tolerate
One familiar culinary or gentle herb first (peppermint, ginger, chamomile, lemon balm). Confirm no allergy and no conflict with your medicines.

### Step 3 — Add at most one or two supportive partners with a related traditional role
- **Aromatic companions** (fennel with mint) for digestive folklore.
- **Soothing mucilage companions** (marshmallow leaf *instead of* overharvested slippery elm when possible).
- **Flavor balancers** (a pinch of cinnamon in golden milk)—flavor is stewardship, not a drug claim.
Avoid mixing many strong actives (laxatives + sedatives + stimulant bitters + EOs) “because more is better.”

### Step 4 — Review cautions as a set, then reassess
Ask: pregnancy? children? blood thinners? surgery soon? alcohol sensitivity? skin integrity? If any answer is uncertain, **simplify to food** or **ask a clinician**. Keep a short use journal (what, how much, how you felt). Stop if you feel worse. Combining herbs does **not** multiply holiness or guarantee benefit—humility is part of wisdom (cf. the spirit of Proverbs’ praise of knowledge with prudence).

## Recipes

### 1. Garden Settling Digestive Tea

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** A mild after-meal aromatic tea in the long kitchen tradition of mint-and-seed digestives—comfort folklore, not a diagnosis of “IBS” or ulcer disease.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 1 tsp dried peppermint leaf (*Mentha × piperita*)
- 1 tsp crushed fennel seed (*Foeniculum vulgare*)
- 8 oz (240 ml) near-boiling water
- Optional: thin slice fresh ginger or a few lemon balm leaves

**Steps:**
1. Combine herbs in a cup or teapot.
2. Pour hot water; cover 10 minutes.
3. Strain; sip warm after a meal.
4. Do not sweeten heavily if you are watching blood sugar—optional honey only for adults/older children per clinician guidance.

**Storage:** Drink fresh. Refrigerate leftovers up to 24 hours; discard if off.

**Cautions:** Peppermint may worsen reflux in some people. Fennel is generally culinary, but medicinal amounts in pregnancy warrant clinician advice; rare Apiaceae allergies exist. Essential oil of peppermint/fennel is **not** a substitute for this tea and is **not** for casual internal use.

**Creation note:** Aromatic garden herbs fit the larger biblical pattern of plants given for food and careful living (Genesis 1:29)—without inventing a “digestive miracle verse.”

### 2. Quiet Evening Lemon Balm & Chamomile Cup

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** A gentle evening cup associated in folk herbalism with calm and settling—not a treatment for anxiety disorders, insomnia disease, or depression.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 1 tsp dried lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*)
- 1 tsp dried German chamomile flowers (*Matricaria chamomilla*)
- 8 oz (240 ml) near-boiling water

**Steps:**
1. Place herbs in a covered mug or pot.
2. Steep 5–10 minutes (chamomile can turn bitter if over-steeped for some palates).
3. Strain; sip in the evening away from screens if you like a wind-down ritual.
4. Optional adult honey drizzle.

**Storage:** Fresh use preferred.

**Cautions:** Chamomile is in the daisy family—avoid if you have strong ragweed/chrysanthemum allergies. Lemon balm may theoretically affect thyroid labs/meds in concentrated use—ask if you have thyroid disease. Sedating stacks with prescription sleep meds need clinician advice. Not a substitute for mental-health care.

**Scripture/creation note:** None forced; gratitude for evening rest is enough.

### 3. Mullein Leaf Respiratory Steam-Cup (tea)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Traditional folk use of mullein leaf as a soothing respiratory-season tea/steam aroma—**not** a pneumonia, COVID, asthma, or tuberculosis treatment.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 1–2 tsp dried mullein leaf (*Verbascum thapsus*), preferably cut-and-sifted
- 8–10 oz water
- Fine strainer or coffee filter (mullein hairs can irritate throats)

**Steps:**
1. Pour hot water over leaf; cover 10–15 minutes.
2. Strain **very well** through a fine filter to remove irritating hairs.
3. Sip warm, or inhale steam cautiously from a safe distance (no burns).
4. Limit to short seasonal folk use unless a clinician advises otherwise.

**Storage:** Fresh.

**Cautions:** Do not use mullein *seeds* or unfiltered fuzzy leaf casually. Asthma and serious breathing problems need medical care—tea is not an inhaler. Pregnancy: ask first. Smoke folklore exists historically; **this recipe does not encourage smoking herbs.**

### 4. Fresh Ginger Root Comfort Tea

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Classic kitchen tea for chilly days and stomach unsettledness folklore—food-level education, not prescription antiemetic therapy.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 3–5 thin slices fresh ginger root (~1–2 tsp grated)
- 8–10 oz water
- Optional: lemon slice; adult honey to taste

**Steps:**
1. Simmer slices gently 10 minutes (decoction-style) or steep grated ginger in near-boiling water 10 minutes.
2. Strain; add lemon/honey if desired.
3. Sip warm.

**Storage:** Fresh; refrigerated leftover tea ~24 hours.

**Cautions:** Ginger can interact with blood thinners and may bother gallstones or reflux in some people—ask a clinician if relevant. Medicinal megadoses in pregnancy belong to clinical guidance, not internet challenges. Ginger essential oil is **not** for casual internal use.

### 5. Rosemary Folklore Clarity Tea (honest limits)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Culinary rosemary tea linked in Mediterranean folklore with “remembrance” and alertness symbolism—**honest limit:** this is not a proven Alzheimer’s drug or IQ booster.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 1 tsp dried rosemary leaf (*Salvia rosmarinus* / *Rosmarinus officinalis*), crumbled
- 8 oz near-boiling water

**Steps:**
1. Steep covered 5–8 minutes (longer gets quite bitter/camphorous).
2. Strain; sip occasionally as a culinary herbal tea.
3. Pair with a walk and good sleep—folklore without magical thinking.

**Storage:** Fresh.

**Cautions:** Avoid large medicinal amounts in pregnancy. Epilepsy or strong camphor sensitivity: ask a clinician. Rosemary **essential oil** is concentrated—aromatic/topical dilute only, **not** oral DIY. People with high blood pressure on meds should clear herbal experiments with a clinician.

**Creation note:** Rosemary is a gift of Mediterranean kitchens; memory folklore is cultural poetry, not a clinical claim.

### 6. Spring Nettle Leaf Food-Like Tea

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Nourishing food-like infusion from dried stinging nettle leaf—mineral-rich folk food tradition, not a “blood cleanser” cure.

**Ingredients:**
- 1–2 tsp dried nettle leaf (*Urtica dioica*) per 8 oz water  
  (or 1 Tbsp per mug for a stronger food-like brew some traditions prefer)

**Steps:**
1. Pour near-boiling water over dried leaf; cover 10–15 minutes (some steep longer for a “nourishing” style—flavor will be deeper/earthier).
2. Strain; drink as a savory green tea.
3. Optional: pinch of mint to soften the grassy taste.

**Storage:** Fresh or refrigerate up to 24 hours.

**Cautions:** Use **dried** or thoroughly cooked leaf—fresh raw nettles sting. May be diuretic; ask if you take blood pressure, diabetes, or blood-thinning medicines, or have kidney disease. Rare allergy possible. Forage only with expert ID (look-alikes exist in some regions).

### 7. Kitchen Turmeric Golden Milk

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** A warm spiced milk drink from South Asian–inspired kitchen tradition—comfort food with turmeric, not an “anti-inflammatory drug” or cancer protocol.

**Ingredients (1 mug):**
- 1 cup (240 ml) milk of choice (dairy or unsweetened plant milk)
- ½–1 tsp turmeric powder (*Curcuma longa*)
- Tiny pinch black pepper (traditional pairing folklore for culinary turmeric)
- Optional: ¼ tsp cinnamon; slice fresh ginger; adult honey to taste

**Steps:**
1. Whisk turmeric (and optional spices) into a splash of cold milk to make a smooth paste.
2. Warm the remaining milk gently—do not scorched-boil.
3. Whisk in the paste; simmer very gently 3–5 minutes.
4. Sweeten lightly if desired; sip warm.

**Storage:** Best fresh; refrigerate leftovers ~24 hours; reheat once.

**Cautions:** Turmeric/curcumin supplements are **not** the same as kitchen powder—high-dose extracts can interact with blood thinners and affect gallbladder disease. Pregnancy: keep to food amounts unless clinician directs. Stains clothing and counters. **Never** present golden milk as an anti-cancer recipe.

### 8. Dark Berries Immune-Season Elderberry Syrup (food tradition)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** A cooked elderberry syrup in the cold-season food tradition of European kitchens—**not** a flu cure, COVID treatment, or vaccine replacement.

**Ingredients (about 2–3 cups syrup, varies):**
- 1 cup dried black elderberries (*Sambucus nigra* ripe berries only) **or** ~2 cups frozen ripe black elderberries
- 3 cups water
- 1 cinnamon stick (optional)
- 3–5 slices fresh ginger (optional)
- 1 cup honey (or to taste; add **off heat**)

**Steps:**
1. Simmer berries (and optional spices) in water about 30–45 minutes until liquid reduces by roughly one-third to one-half.
2. Mash lightly; strain through a fine mesh or cloth (avoid squirting into eyes).
3. Cool until warm, not hot; stir in honey.
4. Bottle in clean glass; label and date; refrigerate.

**Storage:** Refrigerate; typical home teaching often aims to use within **1–3 months**—discard at mold, ferment fizz, or off odor.

**Cautions:** Use only **ripe cooked** black elderberries from trusted *S. nigra* sources. Raw berries, leaves, bark, and other *Sambucus* parts can cause nausea. Autoimmune disease or immunosuppressant drugs: ask a clinician before “immune” folkloric use. **No honey for infants under 1.** Diabetes: sugar load matters. This is food tradition education—not an antiviral drug claim.

### 9. Garlic-in-Honey Folk Preserve (food)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Peeled garlic cloves preserved in honey as a folk food condiment—culinary interest, not an antibiotic replacement.

**Ingredients:**
- Peeled garlic cloves to loosely fill a small clean jar (about 1 cup cloves)
- Enough raw or pasteurized honey to cover completely

**Steps:**
1. Use dry, unblemished cloves; optional light crush.
2. Place in jar; cover fully with honey; release air bubbles with a clean chopstick.
3. Lid loosely at first if very fresh garlic releases gases; then seal.
4. Store cool/dark; cloves soften over days to weeks. Use as a food drizzle/condiment.

**Storage:** Cool pantry or fridge; watch for unusual foaming or mold—discard if suspicious. Some prefer refrigeration for caution.

**Cautions:** Botulism risk conversations exist around low-acid garlic-in-oil; honey-garlic is a different matrix but still requires cleanliness and common sense—**do not give to infants.** Garlic can increase bleeding tendency and interact with HIV meds and anticoagulants—ask if relevant. Food use ≠ treating infection in place of medical care.

### 10. Honey-Onion Household Cough Syrup (food tradition)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Old household sweet onion-and-honey syrup for soothing sore-throat folklore—food tradition, not pneumonia care.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
- Enough honey to cover slices in a jar (about ½–1 cup)

**Steps:**
1. Layer onion in a clean jar; cover with honey.
2. Let sit 2–4 hours (or overnight in the fridge) until syrupy liquid forms.
3. Strain liquid into a clean bottle; discard solids or use in cooking if you wish.
4. Adults: small spoonfuls as a soothing food syrup. Older children: only with clinician/parent judgment; **never under age 1 (honey).**

**Storage:** Refrigerate; use within ~1 week; discard if off.

**Cautions:** Persistent cough, high fever, or breathing trouble → clinician. Honey not for infants. Onion can bother reflux.

### 11. Sunshine Calendula Infused Oil

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Oil infused with calendula petals for external skin-comfort folklore—dry patches and chapped-skin craft, not infection treatment.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 cup dried calendula petals (*Calendula officinalis*)
- 1¼–1½ cups olive oil (enough to cover by ~1 inch)

**Steps (gentle warm method):**
1. Place petals in a clean dry jar; cover completely with oil.
2. Set jar in a warm water bath or yogurt-maker-type warmth **well below frying heat** for several hours, or sunny-window folk method for 1–2 weeks (avoid overheating/spoiling).
3. Shake gently when you remember.
4. Strain through cloth; bottle; label **EXTERNAL** with date.

**Storage:** Cool/dark; often months if dry herb and clean process—discard if cloudy, moldy, or rancid-smelling.

**Cautions:** Daisy-family allergy possible. External only. Do not use in eyes. Deep or infected wounds need medical care.

**Creation note:** Marigold-bright petals are a humble garden mercy for hands that work the soil.

### 12. Calendula Garden Salve

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Beeswax-thickened calendula oil for external chapped-skin craft.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 cup calendula infused oil (Recipe 11)
- ~1 oz (28 g) beeswax (pastilles or grated)—adjust for firmer/softer set
- Optional: 1–2 tsp vitamin E oil as antioxidant folklore for shelf life (not a drug claim)

**Steps:**
1. Gently melt oil + beeswax in a double boiler until just combined.
2. Remove from heat; optional vitamin E.
3. Pour into clean tins; cool undisturbed.
4. Label **EXTERNAL ONLY**, date, ingredients.

**Storage:** Cool/dark; often many months; discard if smell turns rancid.

**Cautions:** Patch-test. Beeswax allergy possible. Not for deep wounds, animal bites, or eye use.

### 13. Aloe Leaf Gel — External Cool Note

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Fresh inner-leaf gel used externally in many cultures to soothe sunburned or irritated skin—educational external note, not burn-unit care.

**Ingredients:**
- 1 mature outer leaf of *Aloe vera* (food-cosmetic grade plant, pesticide-aware)

**Steps:**
1. Rinse leaf; trim spikes.
2. Fillet to expose clear inner gel; avoid yellow latex just under the skin when possible (latex is a stimulant laxative and can irritate).
3. Scoop clear gel; optional brief blend for smoothness.
4. Apply a thin layer **externally** to intact sun-warmed skin; refrigerate leftover gel in a clean jar and use within a few days.

**Storage:** Fridge; few days; discard if pink/brown slimy spoilage appears.

**Cautions:** Patch-test. Do not use on severe burns (seek medical care). Latex orally is **not** recommended here—stimulant laxative risks, pregnancy contraindication in traditional teaching, and regulatory safety concerns. Essential oils do not belong in this gel for casual “stronger” experiments.

**Scripture note:** Biblical “aloes” in some verses likely refer to fragrant eaglewood-type aromatics, **not** identical to modern *Aloe vera* gel—teach the distinction honestly (see Aloes monograph).

### 14. Plantain Leaf Field Poultice

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Classic folk external poultice from broadleaf or ribwort plantain for minor scrapes and insect-bite folklore—first-aid adjacent education, not ER care.

**Ingredients:**
- Several clean *Plantago major* or *P. lanceolata* leaves (positive ID required)
- Clean water; clean cloth

**Steps:**
1. Wash leaves well.
2. Chew briefly **only if** you trust plant ID and your mouth is healthy—or crush with a clean spoon to bruise and moisten.
3. Apply to intact or very minor superficial skin areas; cover lightly with cloth 10–20 minutes.
4. Remove; wash skin; watch for irritation.

**Storage:** Use fresh leaves; do not store a chewed poultice.

**Cautions:** Wrong plant ID is dangerous—do not improvise if unsure. Deep punctures, signs of infection, tick bites of concern, or animal bites → clinician. Do not use mouth-chewed poultices on people immunocompromised without clinical advice.

### 15. Comfrey Leaf External-Only Salve Note

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Teaching entry for **external-only** comfrey craft literacy—and a hard line against internal use.

**Ingredients (educational external pattern):**
- 1 cup dried comfrey leaf (*Symphytum officinale*) infused in olive oil (same method as calendula oil)
- Beeswax to salve consistency (~1 oz per cup oil as a starting point)

**Steps:**
1. Infuse **leaf** oil with dry herb and clean process; strain.
2. Make salve as in Recipe 12.
3. Label boldly: **EXTERNAL ONLY — DO NOT INGEST**.
4. Use sparingly on unbroken skin for traditional bruise/sprain folklore education.

**Storage:** Cool/dark; discard if rancid.

**Cautions (critical):** Comfrey contains **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** linked to serious liver toxicity when taken internally. **No comfrey tea, no comfrey smoothies, no internal tinctures in this curriculum.** Avoid on deep wounds or broken skin where absorption rises. Avoid in pregnancy, nursing, and in children. Prefer PA-reduced professional products if a clinician ever recommends a comfrey-type topical—and still treat as external-only teaching.

### 16. Lavender Linen Sachet & Dilute Topical Note

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Dried lavender for linen fragrance and *highly diluted* topical aroma—not oral essential-oil medicine.

**Ingredients:**
- ½–1 cup dried lavender buds (*Lavandula angustifolia*) for sachets
- For dilute topical (adults): 1 drop lavender essential oil in 1 tsp (5 ml) carrier oil (approximate **1%** ballpark)—or skip EO entirely and rub dried buds in hands for scent

**Steps (sachet):**
1. Fill small cloth bags with dried buds; tie closed.
2. Place in drawer or under pillow (aroma preference varies).
3. Refresh when scent fades.

**Steps (dilute topical optional):**
1. Mix EO into carrier; patch-test inner arm 24 hours.
2. Dab on pulse-point skin if tolerated.
3. Wash hands after; keep away from eyes.

**Storage:** Dried buds airtight/cool; EO bottles dark/cool, childproof.

### 18. Four Aromatics Historical Vinegar (culinary / cleaning-adjacent)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** An original aromatic vinegar in the *spirit* of early-modern “four thieves” folklore—**history literacy + culinary/cleaning-adjacent household use**. It is **not** a plague cure, antiviral drug, or substitute for vaccines, antibiotics, or public-health measures.

**Historical honesty:** Stories of thieves using herbal vinegar during plague years are later folklore layered on real aromatic-vinegar traditions. Recipes varied widely; modern branded products are proprietary. This formula is a **fresh educational reconstruction**, not a copy of any book’s blend.

**Ingredients:**

- 1 Tbsp dried rosemary
- 1 Tbsp dried sage
- 1 Tbsp dried lavender buds
- 1 Tbsp dried thyme
- 2–3 peeled garlic cloves (optional; increases spoilage caution)
- 1 small cinnamon stick (optional)

**Steps:**

**Storage:** Cool pantry if herb-only; refrigerate if garlic was used; use within a few months; discard if mold appears.

**Suggested educational uses:** Salad dressings (diluted with oil); as a kitchen counter wipe **after** proper cleaning for scent (vinegar is not a hospital disinfectant claim). Do **not** drink as a “detox plague tonic.”

### 24. Willow Bark Caution — Prefer Clinician / OTC Aspirin Literacy

**Purpose (educational):** Teach why this curriculum **does not** give a DIY willow-bark tea dosing recipe for pain/fever.

**Why caution instead of a recipe:**
- Willow bark (*Salix* spp.) contains salicylate-related compounds historically linked to aspirin’s story—but **concentration varies** by species, harvest, and preparation.
- Aspirin and salicylates have clear **toxicity, bleeding, Reye’s syndrome (children/teens with viral illness), pregnancy, ulcer, and drug-interaction** issues.
- Measuring bark tea at home is a poor substitute for labeled dose forms.

**Prefer:**
- Clinician guidance for pain/fever.
- Labeled OTC aspirin **only** when appropriate for the person (age, pregnancy status, bleeding risk, viral illness in youth, etc.)—read labels; ask a pharmacist.
- Non-drug comfort measures (hydration, rest) when suitable.

**Do not:** Boil random backyard willow for “natural aspirin.” Do not give salicylate experiments to children. Do not combine willow tea with anticoagulant drugs casually.

**Creation note:** The history of plant → medicine inspires gratitude for both gardens **and** careful pharmacy—not nostalgia that ignores safety.

### 25. Mustard Plaster Historical Education (burn risk — discourage DIY)

**Purpose (educational/historical):** Explain mustard plasters as a **historical** counter-irritant folk practice—and why this curriculum **discourages DIY**.

**Historical sketch:** Ground mustard seed mixed with flour/water was applied externally in older nursing manuals to create heat/redness on the chest or back. Burns, blistering, and pigment changes were known risks even then.

**Why discourage DIY:**
- Mustard’s pungent compounds can **chemically irritate and burn** skin—especially thin skin, children, elders, and people with sensation loss (e.g., some diabetes neuropathies).
- Timing mistakes cause injury.
- Respiratory illness needing oxygen, inhalers, or antibiotics is not solved by a chest burn.

**If studying history only:** Read old texts as history; do **not** recreate on a person. Prefer modern clinical respiratory care and safe warmth (warm socks, humidified air per clinician advice).

**Cautions:** Do not place mustard preparations on children. Do not use if you cannot feel heat normally. Seek care for breathing distress.

### 26. Slippery Elm Inner-Bark Gruel / Tea Note (sustainability-first)

**Purpose (educational/traditional):** Mucilaginous inner-bark gruel/tea appears in North American folk tradition for soothing throat/digestive folklore—taught here with **sustainability first**.

**Sustainability teaching:** Wild slippery elm (*Ulmus rubra*) populations have faced disease and harvest pressure. Prefer:

**If using commercial slippery elm powder (adult food-like note):**

**Storage:** Keep powder dry/airtight; mix fresh.

**Cautions:** May slow absorption of medicines—separate from pills by a couple of hours per pharmacist advice. Pregnancy: ask clinician. Texture can trigger gagging—go slowly. Not a substitute for evaluating GI bleeding, ulcers, or swallowing disorders.
